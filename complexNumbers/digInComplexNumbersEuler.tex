\maketitle    
  



You may be wondering why we represent a complex number in
polar form as $r e^{j\Theta}$ rather than $r\angle\Theta$,
as in your circuits class. In both forms, $r$ is the magnitude
and $\Theta$ is the phase. Euler's formula explains the
exponential notation.

  
  

 Euler's formula relates Cartesian and Polar coordinates for complex numbers.

\begin{eqnarray}
 e^{j \Theta} =  \cos \Theta + j  \sin \Theta
\end{eqnarray}

Geometric interpretation of the Euler's formula is shown below. $z=r ( \cos{\theta} + j \sin{\theta})$, where $r \cos{\theta}=x$ and $r \sin{\theta}=y$. Euler's formula shows that $z=x+jy$ can be represented
in polar form as $z=r e^{j\theta}$, where $r=|z|$.
The expression $e^{j\theta}$ alone has unit magnitude.
You have likely seen this proof in your Calculus class.  {\bf TIP: Your calculator may not know what $e^{j\theta}$ is. Check how to convert between polar and cartesian coordinates on your calculator before the test.}

A sinusoidal signal can have a time-varying amplitude.
For example, multiplying Euler's formula by the envelope
$e^{-\sigma t}$ gives an exponentially damped sinusoid
when $\sigma>0$. Setting $\Theta=\omega t$ gives:


\begin{eqnarray}
e^{-\sigma t}e^{j\omega t}
&=& e^{-\sigma t}\cos(\omega t)
  +j e^{-\sigma t}\sin(\omega t) \nonumber \\
e^{(-\sigma+j\omega)t}
&=& e^{-\sigma t}\cos(\omega t)
  +j e^{-\sigma t}\sin(\omega t)
\end{eqnarray}





Observe the simulation below. On the left side is the complex number $e^{(-\sigma+j\omega)t} = e^{-\sigma t} \cos(\omega t) + j e^{-\sigma t} \sin (\omega t)$ whose value on the real axis represents the real part of the complex number $e^{-\sigma t} \cos(\omega t)$ , and imaginary axis represents the imaginary part of the complex number $e^{-\sigma t} \sin (\omega t)$. On the right side, the real and imaginary parts are plotted
as functions of time.
We use Euler's formula in sinusoidal steady-state circuit analysis.
For a sinusoid $v(t)=A\cos(\omega t+\phi)$, we write
$v(t)=\Re\{\tilde{V}e^{j\omega t}\}$, where the constant
complex amplitude $\tilde{V}=Ae^{j\phi}$ is called a phasor.
The time-dependent expression $e^{(-\sigma+j\omega)t}$
above is a complex signal, rather than a constant phasor.
We will study phasors in the next chapter.
