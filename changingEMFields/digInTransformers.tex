\section{Ideal Transformer}

Transformers consist of two coils of wire on a ferromagnetic core, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:Transformer}. The left coil with an AC generator is called a primary, and the right coil with the resistor is called a secondary. There is no voltage source in the secondary. Transformers are used in many applications, some of which are to electrically decouple circuits, match impedances, and increase or decrease the primary voltage. In an ideal transformer, it is assumed that the total flux produced by the primary will be circulating through the core, and therefore the secondary as well. The induced voltage on the primary and secondary are

\begin{eqnarray}
e_1=-N_1 \frac{d\Phi}{dt} \\
e_2=-N_2 \frac{d\Phi}{dt}
\end{eqnarray}

Choose corresponding winding terminals with the same instantaneous voltage polarity (the terminals normally marked with dots). Measure both voltages from these terminals to their undotted partners. Let $I_1$ enter the primary positive terminal and $I_2$ leave the secondary positive terminal toward the load. These are the reference directions for the following positive ratios; reversing either reference changes the corresponding sign. For ideal coupling, the voltage ratio is

\begin{eqnarray}
\frac{V_1}{V_2}=\frac{N_1}{N_2} 
\end{eqnarray}


An ideal transformer conserves instantaneous power: $v_1(t)i_1(t)=v_2(t)i_2(t)$ with these current references. In sinusoidal steady state, use RMS magnitudes and power factors: $P_1=V_{1,\mathrm{rms}}I_{1,\mathrm{rms}}\cos\phi_1=P_2=V_{2,\mathrm{rms}}I_{2,\mathrm{rms}}\cos\phi_2$. Voltage and current need not be in phase. The ideal transformer current ratio (also valid for consistent phasors) is


\begin{eqnarray}
\frac{I_1}{I_2}=\frac{N_2}{N_1} 
\end{eqnarray}
